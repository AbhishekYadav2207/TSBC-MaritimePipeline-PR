\section{Benchmark Results}
\label{sec:results}

The benchmark examines how encoder choice, record representation, and domain-informativeness affect intrinsic masked-token prediction. The crossed design evaluates seven encoders across five representations and five subsets, with the same sampled records used for matched comparisons. The results show that serialization is a major source of variation, while encoder rankings and diagnostic masking experiments provide complementary evidence for model selection.

\subsection{Representation and Subset Effects}
\label{sec:repr}
\input{tables/table3_marginals}

Table~\ref{tab:marginals} shows clear differences among the five representations. Mixed text achieves the highest marginal Top-1 accuracy, whereas JSON has the lowest. Since the representations are generated from the same occurrence records, this difference reflects how information is presented to the model rather than changes in the source incidents. Combining structured fields with narrative context appears to provide a more favourable prediction setting than the more rigid JSON representation.

The spread across representations is 25.09 percentage points, compared with 3.98 points between the highest- and lowest-performing informativeness subsets. The representation effect is therefore 6.30 times the subset effect. Informativeness remains relevant, but it does not explain the dominant variation observed in this benchmark. Consequently, scores from different serializations should not be treated as directly comparable without accounting for representation; matched comparisons provide the more defensible basis for assessing encoder differences.

\subsection{Encoder Ranking and Robustness}
\label{sec:ranking}
\input{tables/table4_leaderboard}

ModernBERT ranks first in the primary benchmark, with RoBERTa as the nearest competitor (Table~\ref{tab:leaderboard}). ModernBERT's advantage is consistent across most matched conditions, although RoBERTa performs slightly better on the narrative representation. This exception shows that the aggregate ranking does not hold uniformly across all formats. The principal result is therefore an overall preference for ModernBERT under the benchmark protocol, rather than universal dominance in every condition.

The multi-criteria analysis identifies ModernBERT, RoBERTa, SciBERT, BERT-base, and Legal-BERT as non-dominated across the selected objectives. This preserves alternatives where predictive performance must be weighed against tokenizer behaviour and operational cost. In particular, BERT-base offers a smaller parameter footprint and lower measured latency, while SciBERT and Legal-BERT remain competitive under the Pareto analysis. BioBERT and PubMedBERT are dominated within the cohort and objectives evaluated here.

These rankings compare specific pretrained checkpoints rather than isolating the causal effect of domain specialization. Differences in pretraining data, scale, architecture, tokenizer, and training history may all contribute to the observed results. The leaderboard should therefore be interpreted as a benchmark-specific comparison of initialization candidates.

\subsection{Tokenizer Coverage and Tokenization Dynamics}
\label{sec:tokenizer}

Single-token vocabulary coverage does not follow the same ordering as contextual token recovery. BERT-base has the highest coverage in Table~\ref{tab:leaderboard}, yet it does not achieve the strongest Top-1 performance. ModernBERT leads despite greater tokenizer fragmentation (Figure~\ref{fig:repr_tok}b).

The two measures capture different properties: coverage describes how terms are segmented, whereas Top-1 accuracy measures whether the encoder can recover a masked target from context. A term represented by one token is not necessarily easy to predict, and multiple subword pieces may remain predictable from surrounding text. These results support using vocabulary coverage as a diagnostic rather than as a stand-alone selection criterion.

\subsection{Crossed Factorial Statistical Validation}
\label{sec:stats}

The crossed repeated-measures analysis confirms that model identity and representation are the principal sources of variation, with a substantial interaction between them. Model accounts for 55.66\% of total variance, Representation for 25.47\%, and Model $\times$ Representation for 15.34\%. The remaining terms contribute less: Representation $\times$ Subset accounts for 1.12\%, Subset for 0.71\%, and Model $\times$ Subset for 0.59\%; the residual pooled three-way interaction accounts for 1.11\% ($\mathrm{df}=96$). The interaction indicates that the effect of serialization depends on the encoder, while subset selection has a comparatively smaller contribution in this design.

The ANOVA identifies significant effects for Model, $F(6,96)=804.16$, $p<10^{-15}$, $\eta_p^2=0.9805$; Representation, $F(4,96)=552.03$, $p<10^{-15}$, $\eta_p^2=0.9583$; Model $\times$ Representation, $F(24,96)=55.40$, $p<10^{-15}$, $\eta_p^2=0.9327$; Representation $\times$ Subset, $F(16,96)=6.06$, $p=5.25\times10^{-9}$, $\eta_p^2=0.5026$; Subset, $F(4,96)=15.47$, $p=8.46\times10^{-10}$, $\eta_p^2=0.3919$; and Model $\times$ Subset, $F(24,96)=2.12$, $p=0.00533$, $\eta_p^2=0.3469$. These results support the effects observed in the marginal summaries and show that the pattern is not attributable to a single factor alone.

The Friedman test also rejects equal performance across encoders over the matched conditions ($\chi^2=121.17$, $\mathrm{df}=6$, $p=9.27\times10^{-24}$, Kendall's $W=0.8078$). The pairwise results localize the differences: 19 of the 21 comparisons remain significant after Holm correction, while SciBERT--Legal-BERT and BERT-base--Legal-BERT are not distinguishable after adjustment (Table~\ref{tab:pairwise}). Non-significance does not establish equivalence; it indicates that these comparisons do not provide sufficient evidence of a difference under the adopted procedure.

\input{tables/table5_pairwise}

Table~\ref{tab:pairwise} reports paired effects across the 25 shared conditions and Cliff's $\delta$ across unpaired cross-model cell pairs. These statistics answer different questions and should not be conflated. Overall, the inferential results support ModernBERT as the leading candidate within the evaluated cohort, while the representation interaction makes clear that its advantage depends in part on the input format.

\subsection{Masking Sensitivity and Pseudo-Log-Likelihood}
\label{sec:masking}
\input{tables/table6_masking}

The primary benchmark applies random masking to 15\% of tokenizer-defined input units. Table~\ref{tab:masking} reports a separate diagnostic that gives technical terms priority within the same masking budget. As expected from the change in prediction target, Top-1 accuracy declines under domain-aware masking for every encoder. More importantly, the rare-term results produce a different ordering: RoBERTa and SciBERT outperform ModernBERT in the DA-Rare condition. Strong aggregate recovery under random masking therefore does not guarantee equally strong recovery of rare technical terminology. This diagnostic supports evaluating rare-term prediction explicitly rather than relying on the primary leaderboard alone.

Sampled pseudo-log-likelihood provides a complementary measure of token-level conditional likelihood. The corresponding pseudo-perplexities are 13.72 for ModernBERT, 16.02 for RoBERTa, 25.50 for BERT-base, and 91.56 for PubMedBERT. These values are auxiliary to the primary MLM results and are not directly interchangeable with the primary loss or perplexity. The token-type means also differ: domain-token PLL is $-1.64$ for RoBERTa and $-1.95$ for ModernBERT, while general-token PLL is $-2.59$ for ModernBERT and $-2.83$ for RoBERTa. Thus, the diagnostics complement rather than replace the main ranking, showing that model behaviour depends on the token population and masking strategy.

\subsection{Initialization Decision}
\label{sec:decision}

ModernBERT-base was selected for domain-adaptive pretraining because it leads the primary benchmark, remains non-dominated in the multi-criteria analysis, and is supported by the matched-condition statistical comparisons. The decision is based on the combined evidence rather than on MECS alone, since the composite score is a screening heuristic that incorporates both prediction and tokenizer characteristics.

BERT-base remains an alternative where model size and inference cost carry greater weight. The masking diagnostics also identify rare-term recovery as a useful criterion to monitor during adaptation. Taken together, the benchmark provides a structured basis for selecting ModernBERT as the initialization and for evaluating the resulting domain-adapted model.
