\section{Benchmark Results}
\label{sec:results}

The benchmark tests whether intrinsic masked-token performance is shaped primarily by the encoder, the representation used to serialize a maritime record, or the informativeness of the evaluated subset. The 175 matched configurations cross seven encoders with five representations and five subsets, allowing each model to be compared under the same conditions. The results below focus on the patterns that affect encoder selection and on the limits of the conclusions that can be drawn from intrinsic evaluation.

\subsection{Representation and Subset Effects}
\label{sec:repr}
\input{tables/table3_marginals}

The marginal results in Table~\ref{tab:marginals} show a clear separation between the five representations. Mixed text performs best overall, while JSON has the lowest mean Top-1 accuracy. Because the representations are generated from the same occurrence records, this difference reflects the prediction context created by serialization rather than a change in the underlying incidents. Mixed text retains structured information while providing surrounding prose; JSON exposes the same information through a more rigid structure.

The spread across representations is 25.09 percentage points, compared with 3.98 points between the highest- and lowest-performing informativeness subsets. Representation-related variation is therefore 6.30 times larger than subset-related variation in this benchmark. Informativeness still matters, but it does not account for the dominant source of variation observed here.

This finding has a direct consequence for model evaluation: scores obtained from different serializations should not be compared as though the input conditions were equivalent. The matched design reduces this confounding by holding representation and subset constant when comparing encoders. The representation-level means describe the overall formatting effect, while the condition-level results establish how each encoder responds to it.

\subsection{Encoder Ranking and Robustness}
\label{sec:ranking}
\input{tables/table4_leaderboard}

ModernBERT ranks first in the primary benchmark, with RoBERTa as the nearest competitor (Table~\ref{tab:leaderboard}). The same ordering is reflected in the aggregate composite score. These two outcomes are useful for screening, but they are not independent evidence: MECS combines selected benchmark and tokenizer measures, and its separation between the leading encoders is influenced by the fragmentation component described in Section~5.

The paired results provide a more direct comparison. ModernBERT performs better in most matched conditions, although RoBERTa has a small advantage when performance is averaged over the narrative representation. That exception matters because it shows that aggregate superiority does not imply uniform superiority under every serialization. The model-level ranking is best read as an overall preference under the benchmark protocol, not as a claim that one encoder dominates in every setting.

The Pareto analysis also identifies SciBERT, BERT-base, and Legal-BERT as non-dominated alternatives alongside the two leading models. A candidate remains non-dominated when no other model is at least as good on every selected objective and strictly better on one. This preserves meaningful trade-offs beyond Top-1 accuracy alone. In particular, BERT-base has a smaller parameter footprint (110M parameters, compared with 149M for ModernBERT) and a measured latency of 19.51\,ms, making it relevant when inference cost is an important constraint. BioBERT and PubMedBERT are dominated within the objectives and cohort assessed here.

These rankings should not be interpreted as a controlled comparison of general-purpose and domain-specialized pretraining. The evaluated checkpoints differ in training data, scale, architecture, tokenizer, and training history. Their relative scores therefore establish which models are better suited to this benchmark and protocol, not whether domain specialization is inherently beneficial or ineffective.

\subsection{Tokenizer Coverage and Tokenization Dynamics}
\label{sec:tokenizer}

The tokenizer results show that single-token coverage alone is a poor basis for selecting an encoder. BERT-base has the highest single-token coverage in Table~\ref{tab:leaderboard}, yet it does not achieve the strongest Top-1 performance. ModernBERT, by contrast, ranks first despite splitting a larger proportion of evaluated terms into subword pieces (Figure~\ref{fig:repr_tok}b).

This difference reflects what the measures capture. Single-token coverage describes how a tokenizer segments terminology; Top-1 accuracy measures whether the encoder can use context to recover a masked target. A term represented as one token is not necessarily easy for the model to predict, while several subword pieces may remain predictable from their context. The two measures should therefore be considered together. Under the primary masking protocol, contextual recovery provides a different and more useful ranking signal than vocabulary coverage on its own.

\subsection{Crossed Factorial Statistical Validation}
\label{sec:stats}

The crossed repeated-measures analysis supports the patterns in the marginal and encoder-level results. Model identity accounts for 55.66\% of the total variance, representation accounts for 25.47\%, and their interaction accounts for 15.34\%. Thus, performance depends not only on which encoder is used, but also on how the record is presented to it. The remaining effects are smaller: Representation $\times$ Subset accounts for 1.12\%, Subset for 0.71\%, and Model $\times$ Subset for 0.59\%; the residual pooled three-way interaction accounts for 1.11\% ($\mathrm{df}=96$).

The corresponding ANOVA effects are significant for Model, $F(6,96)=804.16$, $p<10^{-15}$, $\eta_p^2=0.9805$; Representation, $F(4,96)=552.03$, $p<10^{-15}$, $\eta_p^2=0.9583$; Model $\times$ Representation, $F(24,96)=55.40$, $p<10^{-15}$, $\eta_p^2=0.9327$; Representation $\times$ Subset, $F(16,96)=6.06$, $p=5.25\times10^{-9}$, $\eta_p^2=0.5026$; Subset, $F(4,96)=15.47$, $p=8.46\times10^{-10}$, $\eta_p^2=0.3919$; and Model $\times$ Subset, $F(24,96)=2.12$, $p=0.00533$, $\eta_p^2=0.3469$. These tests show that the observed effects are not confined to the marginal means alone, while the variance contributions indicate their relative importance in this design.

The Friedman test likewise rejects equal performance across the seven encoders over the 25 matched conditions ($\chi^2=121.17$, $\mathrm{df}=6$, $p=9.27\times10^{-24}$, Kendall's $W=0.8078$). The degree of rank agreement is substantial. The omnibus result establishes that differences exist across models; the pairwise comparisons identify where those differences are clearest.

\input{tables/table5_pairwise}

The pairwise results support ModernBERT's advantage over RoBERTa after Holm correction. The effect is evident across the matched conditions, while the comparison table also shows that the size and consistency of the difference vary by competitor. Most pairwise comparisons remain significant after correction. The exceptions are SciBERT--Legal-BERT ($p_{\mathrm{adj}}=0.170$) and BERT-base--Legal-BERT ($p_{\mathrm{adj}}=0.692$); the evidence does not distinguish these pairs at the chosen threshold, but this should not be taken as proof of equivalence.

The effect-size columns in Table~\ref{tab:pairwise} report different comparisons and should be interpreted accordingly. The paired win/loss counts and paired effect sizes describe the 25 aligned conditions, whereas Cliff's $\delta$ is calculated across unpaired cross-model cell pairs. Keeping these quantities distinct avoids treating an unpaired dominance measure as a matched-condition win rate. Taken together, the statistical analyses support ModernBERT as the leading candidate within the evaluated cohort, with conclusions bounded by the corpus, masking protocol, and matched design.

\subsection{Masking Sensitivity and Pseudo-Log-Likelihood}
\label{sec:masking}
\input{tables/table6_masking}

The primary benchmark uses random masking of 15\% of tokenizer-defined input units. Table~\ref{tab:masking} reports a separate diagnostic that instead prioritizes technical terms within the same masking budget. It covers 28 model--condition configurations across predefined representation--subset conditions and is intended to test sensitivity to the masking target, not to provide a replacement leaderboard.

Top-1 accuracy declines under domain-aware masking for all encoders. The change is especially informative for the rare-term condition, where the ordering differs from the primary benchmark: RoBERTa and SciBERT outperform ModernBERT on DA-Rare, despite ModernBERT's leading aggregate score. This contrast indicates that strong performance on randomly selected masked subwords does not guarantee equally strong recovery of rare technical terms. Rare-term recovery should therefore be treated as a separate evaluation target when selecting and adapting an encoder for maritime text.

Sampled pseudo-log-likelihood provides an additional diagnostic on token-level conditional likelihood. Its pseudo-perplexity values are 13.72 for ModernBERT, 16.02 for RoBERTa, 25.50 for BERT-base, and 91.56 for PubMedBERT; lower values indicate better likelihood under this scoring procedure. These values are not interchangeable with the primary MLM loss or perplexity. The token-type means also differ: domain-token PLL is $-1.64$ for RoBERTa and $-1.95$ for ModernBERT, whereas general-token PLL is $-2.59$ for ModernBERT and $-2.83$ for RoBERTa. These diagnostics qualify the aggregate ranking: the leading model depends in part on which tokens are being evaluated and how they are selected for prediction.

\subsection{Initialization Decision}
\label{sec:decision}

ModernBERT-base was selected as the initialization for domain-adaptive pretraining. This choice is supported by its leading primary benchmark ranking, its position on the Pareto frontier, and the matched-condition statistical comparisons. The decision does not rest on MECS alone; the composite score is a screening heuristic, while the paired results provide direct evidence of performance differences under shared conditions.

BERT-base remains a practical alternative where memory use and inference latency take precedence. The domain-aware masking results also motivate evaluating rare maritime terminology during adaptation rather than relying exclusively on aggregate masked-token accuracy. These considerations inform the next stage of the study, but do not change the primary benchmark ranking.

The conclusion is limited to initialization selection under the present intrinsic evaluation. Masked-token recovery and pseudo-log-likelihood measure prediction behaviour on text; they do not establish downstream gains in named-entity recognition, incident classification, retrieval, or operational decision support. Only ModernBERT underwent DAPT in this study, so selection regret against adapting runner-up encoders remains unmeasured. Downstream evaluation is needed before claiming that this initialization is best for applied maritime tasks.
